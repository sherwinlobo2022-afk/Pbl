Physics Decoded

Six ideas behind the sensors, each with a small live demo. Green badge: the technology is in SpillGuard today. Amber badge: a future concept, not part of the current system. Demos are simplified; numbers use real physics constants.

1 · Photonics: light as a ruler and a wireUsed today

The big ideaLight is the world's fastest stopwatch: flash a pulse, time the echo, and you know how far the car is.
Everyday pictureShout in a canyon and time the echo. LiDAR does that with light, about a million times faster than sound.
Try itDrag the car distance. The echo time is only billionths of a second.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

A laser diode fires a pulse of near-infrared light and a photodiode catches the echo. Light covers about 0.3 m every nanosecond, so a car 15 m away returns the pulse in roughly 100 ns. The distance is d = c·t/2, halved because the pulse travels there and back. Pulses of light in optical fibre carry data the same way, as on/off flashes.

In SpillGuard: the red LiDAR beams in the Simulator (one beam per queue cell), and fibre links where a junction has them for sharing fill levels with neighbours (other links where it does not).

2 · mmWave radar: Doppler at 77 GHzUsed today

The big ideaRadar reads speed from a pitch change, like an ambulance siren sounding higher as it comes at you.
Everyday pictureWaves bounce off a moving car and come back squeezed. The faster the car, the bigger the squeeze.
Try itRaise the speed, then add rain or fog. The reading barely moves because radio waves ignore tiny droplets.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

Radar sends 77 GHz radio waves, wavelength about 3.9 mm. A moving car shifts the returning frequency by fd = 2v/λ, about 7 kHz at 50 km/h, and that shift gives its speed directly. Fog droplets are far smaller than 3.9 mm, so they barely scatter the beam, and even heavy rain costs only around 1 dB over 100 m. Visible and infrared light scatter much more.

In SpillGuard: the purple radar cone and "radar" row in the sensor table. Radar supplies speed and range, and keeps working when fog degrades the camera.

3 · Metals: resistance and the inductive loopUsed today

The big ideaMetal carries current slightly worse when hot, and a steel car body disturbs a road coil's magnetic field.
Everyday pictureElectrons are people crossing a room. Hot atoms jiggle and bump them, so crossing gets slower. A car overhead upsets the coil.
Try itMove the temperature slider and watch resistance climb. Watch the loop react to the car.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

Metals conduct because electrons move freely between atoms. Heat makes the atoms vibrate harder and scatter those electrons, so resistance rises (copper: about +0.4% per °C). An inductive loop is a wire coil in the road. A car's steel body picks up eddy currents that oppose the loop's magnetic field, which lowers the loop's inductance, and the detector sees its oscillator frequency rise.

In SpillGuard: the two yellow loop detectors on each road, which give presence even if the camera and radar fail. Copper wiring and cable heating also follow the resistance rule.

4 · Semiconductors: junctions, light and band gapsUsed today

The big ideaA semiconductor turns light into electricity (camera pixel) or electricity into light (LED), depending on which way you run it.
Everyday pictureThink of a step the electron must climb. Enough light lifts it up. Falling back down gives off light.
Try itSwitch between the two modes and compare what goes in with what comes out.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

In a crystal such as silicon, electrons need a minimum energy, the band gap, to jump into the conduction band. A p-n junction has a built-in electric field. Light with enough energy frees an electron-hole pair and the field sweeps it out as current: a photodiode, and a CMOS camera has one in every pixel. Run the other way, electrons falling across the gap give out light, so an LED or laser diode takes its colour from its gap.

In SpillGuard: the AI camera's CMOS sensor, the photodiode and laser diode inside the LiDAR, the signal-head LEDs, and the chips in the edge computer.

5 · Superconductivity: zero resistance, if cold enoughFuture concept

The big ideaCool some materials enough and current flows with zero loss. Not used in SpillGuard today.
Everyday pictureLike a slope that is rough and sticky above a critical temperature, and perfectly slippery below it.
Try itSlide the temperature down past the critical point and watch resistance drop to zero.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

Below a critical temperature Tc, some materials lose all electrical resistance and push magnetic fields out (the Meissner effect). SQUID sensors use superconducting loops to detect extremely tiny magnetic fields. It needs cooling to near liquid-helium or liquid-nitrogen temperatures. Honest note: it is not used in today's SpillGuard junction hardware, which uses ordinary metals and silicon.

In SpillGuard: nowhere today. It is used elsewhere (MRI magnets, lab magnetometers). Any traffic use would be a long-shot idea, not a plan.

6 · Quantum computing: a future ideaFuture concept

The big ideaA qubit is a coin that is spinning until you look. Quantum computers might one day help plan whole cities. Not used today.
Everyday pictureMeasure it and the coin lands heads or tails, with odds set by the angle.
Try itChange the angle, press Measure ×20 and see results follow the odds. Then raise the junction count to see why planning explodes.
Live numbers
The full science (optional)

A qubit can be in a superposition of 0 and 1 until it is measured, and two entangled qubits give matching results when measured. Setting signal plans across a whole city is a huge combinatorial problem: 40 junctions with just 2 plans each already means over a trillion combinations. Researchers are studying whether quantum methods could help with such optimisation, but no practical advantage for traffic has been shown. SpillGuard uses a classical edge computer today.

In SpillGuard: nowhere today. It is a possible far-future route for city-wide optimisation, beyond the per-junction decisions made now.
